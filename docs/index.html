Machina speculatrix

A 1949 robot that seeks light, shies from glare and finds its way home to recharge, with two valves for a brain. The tortoise below runs the same state machine as the firmware in this repository.

grey-walter-tortoise / after W. Grey Walter, 1948–49 / the controller is the firmware's state machine, held identical by a parity test / source / benmicallef

Between 1948 and 1949, at the Burden Neurological Institute in Bristol, Grey Walter built two small robots to argue a point about brains: that behaviour an observer reads as purposeful needs very few components, provided they sit in a tight feedback loop with the world. Each tortoise had one photocell on a scanning column, one shell that closed a contact when touched, a drive motor, a steering motor, and two valves. From that it sought moderate light, turned away from strong light, butted its way around obstacles, danced in front of a mirror when it caught its own pilot lamp, and went home to a lit hutch when its battery ran low.

The controller running the bodies above is a line-for-line port of the C++ model in the firmware. A parity harness feeds identical input sequences to both and asserts they agree tick for tick, so what you see is what the microcontroller does.

modes

  • scan: darkness. Steering turns continuously, drive at half speed, pilot lamp on.
  • glimpse: a faint rise in light pauses the scan for a moment.
  • lock: moderate light. Steering stops; full drive toward it; pilot off.
  • dazzle: strong light. Steering restarts at half effort: a swerve away.
  • contact: the shell is pushed. The amplifier free-runs and the motors alternate push and steer until the body is clear.
  • docked: on the hutch charger with a flat battery; rests until charged.

The dazzle threshold rides on battery voltage, so a tired tortoise is drawn to the bright hutch it avoided when charged. A pilot lamp lit only while scanning is why two tortoises, or a mirror, produce the halting dance of mutual recognition.

the explorer adaptation

Walter's front wheel carried the photocell, so scanning and travelling happened together. A two-wheel body with a fixed photocell scans by turning on the spot and goes nowhere; the replica therefore adds a short straight run after each full scan. It is labelled as an adaptation, it is off for the 1949 body, and it leaves the light and contact logic untouched.
